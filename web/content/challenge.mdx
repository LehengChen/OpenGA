Formalizing Riemannian geometry is the means here, not the end. What we are really after are a
few open questions about how mathematics is structured, stored, and used once it lives as a
shared, machine-checked graph. The four below are the ones driving the work.

## Formalization as the primary artifact, prose as interface

We think the formal object — the definition, the proof a machine can check — should be the
primary one, and that everything familiar about a piece of mathematics — the prose, the diagrams,
the numbering, even the Lean syntax — is an interface onto it: a view that can be generated,
linked, and replaced.

The reason to invert the usual order is that prose is a lossy interface. A written proof leaves
gaps the reader is trusted to fill, and the gaps sometimes hide mistakes; the machine-checked
version is the one you can actually rely on. Take the formal proof as the source, then, and a
page becomes a rendering of it rather than the thing itself.

That turns the real question into one of interface. If the proof is the artifact, what is the
right way for a human to meet it — prose for one reader, a diagram for another, the dependency
graph for a third, each regenerated from the same source? We are curious what a subject looks
like when every page is just one rendering of the proof underneath. What is still unproven at any
moment is live on the [Status](/docs/status) page.

## How the structure of mathematics navigates its formalization

Mathematics is not a list of results; it is a structure — what rests on what, what generalizes
what, what is dual to what. We want that structure to be more than a description; we want it to be
the map.

In a formal library every result already carries its exact dependencies, so the graph of
what-uses-what is data rather than folklore — and that data can say what to build next, where a
gap is hiding, which interface a hundred later results will end up needing. We are curious how far
the shape of a subject can drive its own formalization, instead of a linear march through chapters.

Part of the question is which relations even deserve to be first-class. `uses` and `implies` are
obvious; `generalizes`, `specializes`, and `dual-to` carry real mathematical content, and a graph
that records them is a richer object than any table of contents.

## How to store mathematics

What is the right substrate for a living body of mathematical knowledge? A corpus of mathematics
is only as valuable as it is composable, and a shelf of PDFs is not.

We keep every statement and every proof as an immutable, content-addressed entry in a hypergraph:
identity is the hash of the content, so references never rot, and any piece can be reused, merged,
or checked on its own — the same idea that makes code addressable by what it is rather than where
it sits, turned on mathematics. The model is laid out in [the data model](/docs/data-model).

We are curious how far the idea carries — what a database *of mathematics itself* should be, and
what becomes possible once a subject has one.

## How humans will do mathematics together

Mathematics has always been a social activity. A proof becomes trusted not the moment it is
written but as people read it, look for holes, reformulate it, and build on it — confidence is
something a community confers over time, not a property of the symbols alone.

Formalization adds something new: when a proof is machine-checked, trusting it no longer depends
on who vouches for it — anyone can run the checker. So the social process does not disappear; it
changes shape.

We are curious what that becomes when the mathematics is a public, machine-checked graph rather
than a shelf of PDFs and a chain of referees. How does a stranger attack the prose for a hidden
hole, defend a proof that it really proves what it claims, or add a piece everyone else can
immediately rely on — alone, and as a community with no center? We think the way people do
mathematics together is about to change, and we want to find out how.

## How we build it

Underneath the questions is a working system, and every part of it is open to improvement — this
is where most concrete contributions land.

- **Visualization** — graph layout that stays legible across thousands of linked results.
- **The reader** — navigation, search, and cross-references that never lose the reader.
- **The toolchain** — extract, register, and audit results as they move between Lean and the store.
- **Integrity** — stable content hashing, deduplication and merge, referential closure as the graph is rewritten.
- **The loop** — make audit → formalize → bridge → re-audit robust and, where possible, automatic.
- **CI and fitness functions** — architectural linters that catch drift before it lands.
