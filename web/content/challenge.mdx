Formalizing Riemannian geometry is the means here, not the end. What we are really after are
a few open questions about how mathematics is structured, stored, and used once it lives as a
shared, machine-checked graph. The four below are the ones driving the work.

## Formalization as the primary artifact, prose as interface

For centuries the textbook *was* the mathematics, and the formal content lived in the
reader's head. We invert that. The formal object — the definition, the proof a machine can
check — is the primary artifact; the prose, the diagrams, the numbering, even the Lean
syntax are interfaces onto it: views that can be generated, linked, and replaced. What does
a subject look like when the proof is the source and every page is just one rendering of it,
and what is the right interface for a human to meet it through? What is still unproven at any
moment is live on the [Status](/docs/status) page.

## How the structure of mathematics navigates its formalization

Mathematics is not a list of results; it is a structure — what rests on what, what
generalizes what, what is dual to what. We want that structure itself to be the map: to say
what to build next, where a gap is hiding, which interface a hundred later results will end
up needing. Can the shape of the subject drive its own formalization, instead of a linear
march through chapters? This is mathematics read as a graph and used as a compass — and the
question of which relations even matter: `uses`, `implies`, `generalizes`, `dual-to`.

## How to store mathematics

What is the right substrate for a living body of mathematical knowledge? We keep every
statement and every proof as an immutable, content-addressed entry in a hypergraph: identity
is content, so references never rot, and any piece can be reused, merged, or checked on its
own. The open question is how far the idea carries — what a database *of mathematics itself*
should be, and what becomes possible once a subject has one. It is the same question Simone
Severini raises as [infrastructure for mathematics](https://simoneseverini.github.io/infrastructure-for-mathematics.html):
a decentralized, content-addressed ecosystem whose atoms are the mathematical artifacts
themselves, not the papers about them. Our model is described in [the data model](/docs/data-model).

## How humans will do mathematics together

Mathematics has always been a social activity. A proof becomes trusted not the moment it is
written but as people read it, look for holes, reformulate it, and build on it — confidence
is something a community confers over time, not a property of the symbols alone. De Millo,
Lipton, and Perlis made this argument sharply in 1979, and Thurston put it at the center of
how the subject actually advances: what mathematicians produce is human understanding, not
just formal derivations.

That social process can scale, and it can decentralize. The [Polymath](https://www.nature.com/articles/461879a)
experiments showed that hard theorems can be attacked in the open by a loose crowd with no
central authority. Formalization adds something new: when a proof is machine-checked, trusting
it no longer depends on who vouches for it — anyone can run the checker. The Lean
[mathlib](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09336) community is already thousands of people
maintaining one verified library together, which a decade ago would have sounded implausible.

The open question is what this becomes when the mathematics is a public, machine-checked graph
rather than a shelf of PDFs and a chain of referees. The social process does not disappear; it
changes shape. How does a stranger attack the prose for a hidden hole, defend a proof that it
really proves what it claims, or add a piece everyone else can immediately rely on — alone, and
as a community with no center? We think the way people do mathematics together is about to
change, and we want to find out how.

## How we build it

Underneath the questions is a working system, and every part of it is open to improvement —
this is where most concrete contributions land.

- **Visualization** — graph layout that stays legible across thousands of linked results.
- **The reader** — navigation, search, and cross-references that never lose the reader.
- **The toolchain** — extract, register, and audit results as they move between Lean and the store.
- **Integrity** — stable content hashing, deduplication and merge, referential closure as the graph is rewritten.
- **The loop** — make audit → formalize → bridge → re-audit robust and, where possible, automatic.
- **CI and fitness functions** — architectural linters that catch drift before it lands.
