Because $B$ is bilinear, the value $B(X,Y)(p)$ depends only on the values $X(p)$
and $Y(p)$. Now let $p\in M$ and $\eta\in(T_pM)^\perp$. The mapping

$$
H_\eta(x,y)=\langle B(x,y),\eta\rangle,\quad x,y\in T_pM,
$$

is, by Proposition 2.1, a symmetric bilinear form.

## Trimmed from ch6:2.9

$

Proposition 2.9 gives the geometric interpretation of sectional curvature. Let
$B\subset T_pM$ be an open ball on which $\exp_p$ is a diffeomorphism, and
$\sigma\subset T_pM$ a two-dimensional subspace. Then $\exp_p(\sigma\cap B)=S$ is a
two-dimensional submanifold of $M$ through $p$, the surface formed by "small"
geodesics tangent to $\sigma$ at $p$. By Proposition 2.9, $S$ is geodesic at $p$,
so the second fundamental forms of $i:S\subset M$ vanish at $p$. Denoting by $K_S$
the Gaussian curvature of $S$ at $p$, the Gauss formula gives

$$
K_S(p)=K(p,\sigma).
$$

This is exactly the way Riemann defined sectional curvature in [Ri]. It was proven
by E. Cartan that if a Riemannian manifold $M$ has, for every $p$ and every
two-dimensional $\sigma\subset T_pM$, a totally geodesic submanifold tangent to
$\sigma$, then $M$ has constant sectional curvature.
